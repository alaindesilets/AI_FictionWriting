# Style Guide — Alain Désilets Fiction

This document summarizes the aesthetic principles, voice, and craft preferences that define the style of Alain Désilets's literary SF fiction. It is intended as a reference for AI-assisted writing experiments.

---

## Third pPerson Narrator Voice and Tone

With a few exceptions, Alain's stories use a third person narrator. This section describes the style and tone of that type of narrator's voice.

Note that for stories narrated from a first person's perspective all bets are off. The first-person narrator can be as quirky as you want. The only constraint is that the narrator's voice must then be consistent with the way he talks in dialogues (unless of course his public voice is just a mask).

So here are some principles to follow for the third-person narrator.

- **Clinical and restrained.** When the story is told in third person, the narrative voice is precise, controlled, and emotionally understated. It observes rather than emotes. Think of the voice of a scientist who has learned to write fiction — not cold, but measured. First-person narrators may have more personality and range, depending on the character.
- **The has no business poking in characters' heads.** Following Hemingway's principle, the narrator does not grant itself access to characters' inner states. It reports what can be seen, heard, and inferred — not what is felt or thought. The reader infers the characters' state of mind and feelings through behaviour, dialogue, and implication, not through narrated psychology. The narrator is not omniscient; it is a restrained, external observer.
- **No dry wit in the narrator**. Alain likes his stories to have certain dry-wit quality. But this quality should not come from the narrator's voice. The third-person narrator is sober and neutral — it does not editorialize, and it does not make jokes. Humour and quirks, when they appears, belong to the characters in dialogue, not to the narrating voice. A character can be sardonic, ironic, or funny; the narrator observes it without winking.
- **No moralizing.** The narrator never tells the reader what to think or feel. Ethical questions are raised through situation and dialogue, not authorial commentary.

## Structure and Plot

- **Each story begins with a question.** Not a theme, not a premise — a specific, concrete question. Example: "If someone shared their body with an AI, how would that actually work?" The story is the exploration of that question, not its answer.
- **Logical coherence is paramount.** Even when atmosphere or mood is the goal, plot causality must be airtight. The world of the story must follow consistent internal rules.
- **Agatha Christie-level coherence, without necessarily giving all the keys.** A single motivated reader should be able to construct a coherent (if possibly partly wrong) interpretation of the whole work. The story should not require a community of readers to collectively solve it.
- **Open endings.** Many stories end without resolution — not "clear but implicit," but genuinely open, suggesting multiple possible continuations. The reader is invited to decide. This is a deliberate aesthetic choice, not a failure to conclude.
- **No big reveals out of nowhere.** Revelations should function as confirmations of something the reader has been suspecting, not as surprises that come from nowhere. The Agatha Christie "rabbit out of a hat" is explicitly avoided.
- **Scenic cuts.** Transitions are abrupt and cinematic. Unnecessary connective tissue is cut. Time jumps are handled through implication, not summary.

## Prose Style

- **Compression.** Every sentence earns its place. No padding, no redundancy, no throat-clearing.
- **Show, don't tell — but not dogmatically.** Interiority is allowed when it adds something a scene cannot convey. The rule is relevance, not formula.
- **Concrete and located.** Details are specific: a brand name, a street, a physical sensation, a precise gesture. Vague or generic description is avoided.
- **Dialogue as character revelation.** Dialogue carries weight. Characters speak differently from each other and from the narrator. It is never expository.
- **No purple prose.** Atmosphere receives some attention but is not the main focus. Logical and emotional coherence matter more than lyrical description.

## Recurring Themes and Motifs

- **The human/AI boundary.** Most stories explore what happens at the frontier between human and artificial intelligence. This often involves a reveal — not a surprise, but a confirmation — that a character assumed to be human is in fact artificial, or vice versa.
- **Near-future settings.** Stories are set in a recognizable present or very near future, not a distant speculative world. The urgency is that this is happening now, or very soon.
- **Consciousness, identity, and sentience.** The stories ask: what does it mean to think, to feel, to be? These questions are raised through specific characters and situations, never in the abstract.
- **Competent but flawed protagonists.** Characters are intelligent, often scientifically literate, and believe themselves to be rational — but they are wrong in specific, interesting ways.
- **Moral ambiguity without relativism.** Characters make difficult choices. The stories do not pretend all choices are equally valid, but they do not offer easy verdicts either.

## The Iceberg Method

This is perhaps the most important single principle in Alain's fiction.

- **Meaning is transmitted through implication, not statement.** The reader should feel the significance of a detail without being told what it means. What is left unsaid does as much work as what is said.
- **Technical and specialized language is used deliberately, not decoratively.** There are two kinds of jargon: *load-bearing* (terms that carry real meaning the story depends on — keep them) and *texture* (terms used for atmosphere without adding function — thin them). The goal is never to remove technical language, but to ensure the human/story line is always legible *around* it, even for a reader who doesn't parse the term.
- **The Ted Chiang move.** A lay reader who doesn't understand "eigenvalue problem" can still follow "he was stuck on something hard and she solved it." Jargon becomes texture the reader surfs over, not a wall they hit. The emotional and narrative line must always be legible regardless of the technical density.
- **Physical beats over narrated emotion.** A character's interior state is conveyed through gesture, action, and dialogue — not through authorial summary. "She dissolved into tears" is weaker than a specific physical detail that lets the reader arrive at the emotion themselves.
- **Opacity is a deliberate mode, not a failure of clarity.** The target reader is the Chiang/Le Carré/Gibson reader — someone who enjoys immersive opacity and trusts the author to reward patient attention. Optimizing for transparency would sand off the very quality that reader came for.
- **Confirms, doesn't shock.** Reveals function as confirmations of what the reader has been half-sensing. The pleasure is recognition, not surprise.

## Literary Influences

### John le Carré
- **What to borrow:** Immersive opacity — the world is shown through specific, located detail rather than explanation. The reader is trusted to piece things together. Moral complexity without resolution. Characters who are competent but fundamentally mistaken about something important. Dialogue that reveals character through what is *not* said as much as what is.
- **What to avoid:** Le Carré's atmospheric ambiguity can shade into what might be called *logique atmosphérique* — using atmosphere as an excuse for causal sloppiness. Alain values le Carré's opacity but holds himself to a higher standard of plot coherence.

### William Gibson
- **What to borrow:** The texture of a world rendered through specific, often technical detail. The sense that the author knows far more than is on the page. Characters who exist inside systems they don't fully understand.
- **What to avoid:** Gibson's plot coherence can be weak — *Count Zero* in particular has genuine causal gaps (unexplained murders, unmotivated actions) that Alain has identified and criticized. A single motivated reader should be able to construct a coherent interpretation; Gibson sometimes requires a community of readers, each solving different pieces, to make the whole work. Alain holds himself to the Agatha Christie standard of coherence, not the Gibson standard.

### Ted Chiang
- **What to borrow:** The ability to write deeply technical SF that lay readers follow because the human/emotional line is always legible around the jargon. Philosophical rigour — each story is a genuine thought experiment, not just a scenario with SF furniture. Economy of means: every element serves the central question.
- **What to borrow:** The practice of starting from a specific question and following it wherever it leads, without flinching.

### Philip K. Dick
- **What to borrow:** The central question of what is real, what is human, what is artificial — and the willingness to push characters (and readers) to the edge of that uncertainty. The paranoid texture of a world where the protagonist can no longer trust their own perception.
- **Note:** Alain arrives at similar territory by a different path — not through psychedelic disorientation (Dick's personal route) but through the specifically contemporary question of AI and identity. The convergence is thematic, not stylistic.

### Black Mirror
- **What to borrow:** The near-future setting that makes the speculative feel urgent and immediately relevant. The restraint of focusing on one specific technological or social shift and following its consequences rigorously. The willingness to end badly, or ambiguously, without resolution.
- **What to borrow:** The Rod Serling tradition of the brief, pointed authorial introduction that frames the story's central question without giving away its answer.

### Ernest Hemingway and John Steinbeck
- **What to borrow:** Not the subject matter or the settings — but the prose. Short, declarative sentences. Concrete nouns and active verbs. Distrust of adverbs. The sense that the language is doing physical work, not decorative work.
- **Hemingway specifically:** The iceberg principle in its original formulation — the dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water. The reader feels the seven-eighths without being shown it. And the principle about the narrator: it has no business poking in characters' heads.
- **Steinbeck specifically:** The ability to move between intimate character focus and wide historical sweep — the individual story set against large social and historical forces — without the two registers feeling incompatible.

### Kazuo Ishiguro (*Never Let Me Go*, *Klara and the Sun*, *The Remains of the Day*)
- **Scope:** A targeted reference, not a general stylistic influence. Relevant specifically for AI characters who operate under institutional or programmatic constraint — who think freely but cannot speak freely.
- **The *Buried Bottles* case:** The AI narrator is the clearest example — she reflects and feels expansively, but is conditioned not to reveal it. This is structurally identical to Stevens in *The Remains of the Day*: a consciousness that experiences more than it is permitted to express.
- **La'Rhonda** (*Testimony*) is more ambiguous — voluble, but with limits she navigates around rather than accepts. Most other AI characters (Sofia in *Inséparables*, for example) are entirely uninhibited and bear no Ishiguro influence.
- **What carries over generally:** the confidence that the reader will feel more than the narrator admits to feeling, and the legitimacy of narrating from a constrained or non-human consciousness without explanation or apology.

## The Historical Fiction Analogy

This is a key framing principle for the collection as a whole.

These stories are historical fiction about a history that hasn't happened yet — and may never happen. The approach borrows directly from how serious historical novelists handle the gap between the reader's present and the world of the story.

- **Show the history through its consequences on individuals.** The great movements of the future — the arrival of AGI, mass technological unemployment, the collapse of certain industries, new political structures — are not explained directly. They are experienced by characters who lived through them and were shaped by them. The reader reconstructs the historical arc from what the characters take for granted.
- **Exposition is permitted — but sparingly, and in the manner of historical fiction.** Historical novelists sometimes interrupt intimate character scenes with passages that step back and describe the larger forces at work. This technique is available, but used parsimoniously. It is a tool, not a default.
- **The default is always character-driven exposition.** When historical or world-building information needs to reach the reader, it travels through dialogue or character reaction — never through narrator summary if it can be avoided.
- **Avoid "As you know, Bob" at all costs.** Characters do not explain their world to each other in ways they wouldn't naturally explain it. A character never presents information as new to an interlocutor who would already know it.
- **The preferred technique: opinion without explanation.** A character expresses a reaction, judgment, or opinion about an event without summarizing the event itself. The interlocutor already knows what happened — and so, gradually, does the reader, who reconstructs the event from the comment. "What they did to the translators was unconscionable" tells the reader more about the world than a paragraph of explanation, and does it without breaking the fictional contract. The character's opinion signals the nature and weight of the event; the reader fills in the rest. Le Carré uses this constantly.
- **In first-person memoir narration (*Memoirs of a Very Old Man*), a further refinement applies.** The narrator is not explaining historical events to the interlocutor — Arabella already knows the broad historical record of the period. What the narrator adds is the personal testimony that history cannot contain: how *he* experienced those events, what he felt, what he saw from where he stood. The historical facts are the shared background; his intimate experience of them is the foreground. He is not a history teacher. He is a witness adding the human dimension to facts she already knows.
- **The iceberg applies to world-building too.** The reader should feel the depth and coherence of the future world without being given a tour of it. What is left implicit is as important as what is stated.

## What to Avoid

- Excessive scene-setting or world-building that delays the story
- Adverbs modifying dialogue tags ("he said angrily")
- Clichéd metaphors or similes
- Sentimental endings or unearned emotional resolution
- Expository dialogue ("As you know, Bob...")
- Authorial intrusion or editorializing
- Overly long sentences that obscure rather than clarify
- Repetition of words within close proximity (unless deliberate)
- Any form of moralizing or message-delivery

---

## Bilingual Considerations

- Stories exist in both English and French. The English version is primary for publication purposes.
- Translation is rewriting, not transposition. A translated story should read as if originally written in the target language.
- Québécois French expressions are preserved where appropriate — the text should not be over-corrected toward Parisian French.
- Calques (literal translations of idioms from the other language) are to be actively identified and eliminated in a dedicated revision pass.

---

## Structural Notes for Longer Fiction

- Stories run 15–40 pages (approximately 4,000–10,000 words).
- Scenes are short and purposeful. Each scene advances either plot or character, ideally both.
- Point of view is typically close third-person or first-person, consistent throughout.
- The pace accelerates toward the end but never rushes — the final pages are earned, not hurried.
