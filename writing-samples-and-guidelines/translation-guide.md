# Translation Guide — Alain Désilets Fiction

This guide is Claude AI's best understnanding of the principles that Alain Désilets explained to it, while translating his short story *Shooting the Breeze* / *Parler pour parler*.. It is intended as a reference for AI-assisted translation of the author's fiction.

It is written in terms of translsation from English to French because it is the most common situation. But most of the principles provided here also apply to French to English translation.

Many of the principles listed here are things that Alain learned from Louise Brunette, a close friend, and seasoned professional translator and revisor.

---

## Foundational Principle

**Translation is rewriting, not transposition.**

A translated story should read as if it were originally written in the target language. The goal is never to produce a faithful rendering of the English sentence — it is to find the French sentence that does the same work. When these two objectives conflict, the French sentence wins.

## Process: Three Phases (after Louise Brunette)

**The core problem to avoid: double blindness (*double aveuglement*).**
A first draft that follows the source text too closely creates a double blindness: the translator is blinded simultaneously by the English original and by a French text that mirrors it too faithfully. This makes it very difficult to gain critical distance from the source language. The resulting text reads as translated prose, not as French prose.

The solution is to invert the usual priority: produce quality French first, verify fidelity to the English second.

---

**Phase 1 — Preparation.** Read the English text several times. Do not begin translating. Instead, identify:
- Expressions and idioms that will be difficult to render in French
- Linguistic traps the English text sets — constructions that will naturally pull toward calque
- Passages where the English rhythm or sentence architecture is particularly strong and likely to contaminate the French

Take notes. Build a preliminary terminology sheet for technical or recurring terms.

**Phase 2 — French redaction.** Work paragraph by paragraph. For each paragraph:
1. Read it several times until you have it firmly in mind.
2. Set the English aside.
3. Write the French paragraph entirely from memory.
4. Resist the temptation to look back at the English paragraph after completing the French version. Re-read the French paragraph as many times as needed to polish it — but evaluate it on its own merits as French prose, not against the English. Once satisfied, move on to the next paragraph.

By writing from memory rather than by transcription, you cannot lean on the English sentence structure as scaffolding. What comes out in French is the *meaning* reconstructed in French — not the English sentence disguised in French words. Memory acts as a natural filter against the source language.

Do not consult the English systematically until Phase 3.

**Phase 3 — Fidelity verification.** Read the French and English side by side. Identify any significant divergences from the original — missed meaning, altered emphasis, lost nuance. Correct these divergences without sacrificing the quality of the French prose. This is the only phase where the English is consulted systematically. The rule: when a correction is required, find a French solution, not an English one transplanted into French.

## The Calque Problem

A **calque** is a structural borrowing from the source language — a French sentence that is grammatically correct but follows English sentence architecture rather than French. Calques are the primary enemy of literary translation.

Common categories in English → French translation:

**Syntactic calques**
- English frequently uses noun strings: "a risk assessment algorithm" → not "un algorithme d'évaluation de risque" (acceptable) but watch for longer chains that require restructuring
- English prefers active constructions; French tolerates more varied syntax — restructure when the English pattern feels forced in French

**Idiomatic calques**
- English idioms translated literally: "take it easy" → not "prends-le facilement"
- Phrasal verbs rendered word-for-word
- Test: would a native French speaker write this sentence, or does it smell of translation?

**Rhythmic calques**
- Sentence length and rhythm differ between English and French literary prose
- French literary sentences tend to be longer and more periodic than English ones; but English short sentences can be effective in French too — the point is that the rhythm should be *chosen*, not inherited

**Punctuation calques** — see dedicated section below

## Punctuation

**Em dashes (—)**
Em dashes are a specifically English construction. French does not use them in the same way or with the same frequency. Every em dash in the English original must be reviewed individually.

Options, in order of preference:
- Parentheses, for asides and clarifications: *Cette posture ne servait à rien (les robots ne se fatiguent pas), mais...*
- Two-point separation into shorter sentences: *Cette posture ne servait à rien. Les robots ne se fatiguent pas. Mais...*
- Comma restructuring when the aside can be integrated naturally
- A single em dash retained only when the rupture effect is emotionally meaningful and deliberate — rare

**Quotation marks**
Use French guillemets « » throughout, not English "quotation marks". Apply French typographic conventions: space after « and before ».

**Ellipsis**
French uses … (three dots, no space before) where English uses ... — functionally equivalent but verify spacing conventions.

## Register and Market

The target is **both the Quebec and French markets simultaneously**. This creates occasional tension that must be navigated deliberately:

- **Quebec idioms** that are transparent in France: keep them. The text should feel authentically Quebec without being opaque to a French reader. A French reader who encounters "parler pour parler" or "comme de rien" will reconstruct the meaning from context.
- **Quebec idioms** that are genuinely opaque to a French reader: evaluate case by case. Some opacity is acceptable and even desirable as a marker of the author's identity. Excessive smoothing toward Parisian French would sand off something real.
- **Do not over-correct toward Parisian French.** The author is a Quebec writer. The text should not pretend otherwise.
- **Dialectal markers in dialogue**: characters who speak Quebec French should retain their voice. Do not neutralize regional speech into standard French.

---

## Vocabulary Decisions

Establish and maintain a **terminology sheet** for each story. Key categories:

**Technical terms**
- AI and technology terms: prefer established French equivalents when they exist (réseau neuronal, apprentissage profond, apprentissage automatique). Note that French journalists and writers in France tend to anglicize more readily than Quebec usage does — follow Quebec preference for French terms where they exist, and anglicize only when no established French equivalent is in common use.
- When in doubt, consult French-language AI journalism and research from Quebec sources (e.g. Radio-Canada science coverage) rather than French sources, which tend toward anglicization.
- Canonical texts: when characters quote or reference an established text (e.g., Asimov's Three Laws), use the canonical published French translation, not a new translation of the English.

**Character names**
- Generally kept in English/original form unless the story was originally written in French
- Robot or AI names that are functional (e.g., "CropGards") may be adapted to function in both languages ("AgriBots")

**Titles**
- Story titles are creative decisions, not translations. The French title should do the same work as the English title, not mirror its structure. *Shooting the Breeze* → *Parler pour parler* (not *Tirer sur la brise* or any literal equivalent).

## Dialogue

Dialogue has different conventions in French:

- French uses guillemets « » for dialogue, not quotation marks
- Long exchanges often use em dashes (tirets) at the start of each speaker's line, without closing punctuation — this is a legitimate French convention for extended dialogue, distinct from the em dash problem described above
- Dialogue tags: French has more flexibility than English but also more traps — avoid calqued constructions like *il dit* repeated identically

The author's dialogue is a primary carrier of character voice. When translating dialogue, prioritize the **rhythm and register of the character's speech** over literal equivalence. A character who speaks terse, clipped English should speak terse, clipped French — not grammatically expanded French.

---

## Specific Problem Areas

**"There is / there are" constructions**
English uses "there is/are" constantly. French "il y a" is correct but overused in translation. Look for restructured French sentences that avoid it: *Une pyramide de canettes ornait l'arrière de son bureau* rather than *Il y avait une pyramide de canettes...*

**Gerunds and present participles**
English uses gerunds extensively ("walking down the street, he noticed..."). French equivalent constructions can feel heavy. Often better to restructure as two independent clauses.

**Passive voice**
English uses passive voice more freely than French literary prose. Restructure toward active where possible.

**Adjective placement**
French adjective placement (before or after noun) changes meaning and rhythm. Do not default to post-nominal placement for all adjectives — place deliberately.

## Quality Evaluation Criteria

After Phase 2, evaluate the translation against these questions:

1. Read aloud: does it sound like French prose, or like translated text?
2. Are there any sentences a native French speaker would not have written?
3. Are all em dashes resolved?
4. Is the terminology consistent throughout?
5. Does the dialogue retain the character's voice and register?
6. Are Quebec idioms present where appropriate, without tipping into opacity?
7. Does the title work as a French title, independent of the English original?
8. Are canonical texts (quoted laws, song lyrics, famous passages) using their published French translations?

## A Warning About "French Literary Style"

When instructed to translate "as if the text were originally written in French," do not interpret this as an invitation to adopt the ornate, erudite prose style common in French literary tradition — long, complex sentences, elevated vocabulary, conspicuous display of literary learning.

The author writes in a deliberately Anglo-Saxon style, even in French: short sentences, simple words, direct and concrete, no flourishes. This is a conscious aesthetic choice, influenced by Hemingway and Steinbeck (see the style guide), and it applies equally to the French versions of his stories.

"Written as if originally in French" means: idiomatic, natural, free of calques — not: elaborate, formal, or stylistically inflated. The target register is closer to a well-written Quebec literary short story than to a Paris literary novel.

When in doubt: shorter is better. Simpler is better. If a word choice feels impressive, look for a plainer one.

## French → English Specifics

This section covers challenges specific to translating the author's French-original stories into English. It supplements the main translation guide, which addresses English → French. The three-phase process (preparation, redaction from memory, fidelity verification) and the double-blindness principle apply equally in this direction.

---

### Structural Differences That Create Friction

**Sentence rhythm**
French literary sentences, even in the author's direct style, tend toward more elaborate subordination than their English equivalents. What reads naturally as a single French sentence often works better as two English sentences. Resist the temptation to preserve French sentence architecture in English — it will read as translated prose.

**Negation**
French uses double negation (*ne…pas*, *ne…jamais*, *ne…rien*) where English uses a single negative form. "Il ne savait pas" → "He didn't know" (not "He did not know not"). Watch for *ne* that appears without its partner in informal register — Quebec French frequently drops *ne* in speech (*"Je sais pas"*) — and render accordingly in English dialogue.

**Pronoun subjects**
French requires an explicit subject pronoun where English can omit or vary. Conversely, French verb conjugations often carry more information than English equivalents, allowing the subject to be inferred. When translating dialogue, the resulting English may need more explicit attribution than the French to remain clear.

**Articles and determiners**
French uses articles more extensively than English. *"Le béryllium est rare"* → "Beryllium is rare" (not "The beryllium is rare"). Partitive articles (*du, de la, des*) often disappear entirely in English.

---

### Register and Vocabulary

**Quebec idioms going into English**
Quebec French idioms that have no English equivalent — *en dedans de*, *à date*, *en quelque part*, *checkez ça* — require creative solutions rather than literal translation. Find an English idiom of equivalent register and flavour, not a dictionary gloss. The goal is a character who sounds natural in English, not a character who sounds like they're speaking French in translation.

**Profanity and sacres**
Quebec French sacres (*ostie*, *câlisse*, *tabarnak*, *merde*) have no exact English equivalents. They carry a specific cultural weight — religious in origin, intensified by taboo — that English profanity doesn't replicate. Practical approach: match the register and intensity, not the literal source. A strong sacre in context warrants a strong English expletive; a mild one warrants something milder. Avoid translating sacres as the equivalent French-origin English words ("merde" → "shit" is reasonable; "ostie d'shit" → "holy shit" captures the compound intensifier better than a literal rendering).

**Technical vocabulary**
French AI and technology terms that the author prefers (réseau neuronal, apprentissage profond, apprentissage automatique) translate back to their established English equivalents (neural network, deep learning, machine learning). No creativity needed here — use standard English technical vocabulary.

---

### Dialogue Conventions

French dialogue punctuation differs from English:
- Replace guillemets « » with English quotation marks " "
- French dialogue dashes (tirets) at the start of each speaker's line become standard English dialogue formatting with quotation marks and dialogue tags
- French allows longer untagged exchanges than English convention does — add dialogue tags where needed for clarity without over-tagging

**Spacing**
French inserts a non-breaking space before certain punctuation marks ( ; : ! ? ) and inside guillemets. These spaces disappear entirely in English. Remove them; do not substitute a regular space.

## What Does Not Transfer

**Grammatical gender**
French grammatical gender creates agreement patterns (adjective endings, past participle agreement with avoir, reflexive verb agreement) that simply do not exist in English. These are not translated — they dissolve. Be alert to cases where gender is narratively significant (a gendered pronoun revealing character identity) versus cases where it is purely grammatical. The former requires a creative English solution; the latter simply disappears.

**Formal/informal address (tu/vous)**
The tu/vous distinction carries social meaning that English does not encode grammatically. When this distinction matters to a scene — a character switching from vous to tu as a gesture of intimacy, or maintaining vous as a marker of distance — find a way to signal it in English through register, word choice, or an explicit narrative note. When it is purely conventional (a character addressing a stranger as vous), it disappears without loss.

**Verb tenses**
French literary prose uses the passé simple (literary past) where English uses the simple past. These are functionally equivalent for translation purposes. More complex is the distinction between passé composé and passé simple — in French literary prose, the passé composé can signal a different relationship to the action (more immediate, more connected to the present) than the passé simple. In English both become simple past. When this distinction matters to the narrative voice, find English solutions (tense variation, adverbials, proximity markers) rather than trying to encode the French tense system directly.

## The Double-Blindness Problem in Reverse

The same double-blindness risk applies when translating French → English: a first-pass English draft that mirrors the French too closely will read as translated prose. Apply the same paragraph-by-memory technique — read the French paragraph until you have it in mind, then write the English from memory without looking back at the French until the paragraph is satisfying on its own terms as English prose.

The specific contamination risk in this direction: French subordination patterns bleeding into English, producing long, comma-heavy sentences that feel vaguely European. If a sentence feels elegant in a way that makes you pause, check whether the elegance is French syntax wearing English clothes.
