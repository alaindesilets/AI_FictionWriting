# Memoirs of a Very Old Man: Story premise

## Overview

**Working title:** *Memoirs of a Very Old Man*

This is an intimate, reflective science-fiction story, in the form of a memoir written by an extremely old man, for the benefit of his favorite great-(possibly great?)-granddaughter Arabella. 

It explores what happens to human meaning when AI and robotics make human labor economically irrelevant, and what it means to remain human when technology offers progressively less embodied forms of existence.

The story is deliberately more personal and introspective than the author's usual higher-stakes third-person science fiction stories. It is intended as an experiment in warm, first-person narration and in the diary/memoir/found-document form.

## World Premise

In the future, AI and robotics can provide intelligence and physical labor at negligible marginal cost.

Human jobs have essentially disappeared. People are effectively **"born retired."**
Basic material needs are guaranteed, but the world has not become completely post-scarcity. 

çPhysical resources, locations, experiences, and other goods remain scarce. Some mechanism—potentially based on equal allocations of credits and bidding/auction-like allocation—determines who gets access to scarce resources.

The central social question is therefore no longer:
> How do we keep humans economically useful?
Instead, it becomes:
> What makes a human life meaningful when economic usefulness has disappeared?

The story contrasts the old man's generation, which found meaning partly through work and contribution, with descendants who never had to work for a living. 

It also explores how the change came about. The writer of the memoir (who is never named), was in his prime at the time of the change. Maybe he even played a small role in it (ex: he was an AI researcher, but not necessarily one of the prime movers and shakers of the time).

## Work, Meaning, and Fulfillment

The old man does not necessarily regard the new world as a mistake. He enjoys retirement and recognizes the enormous benefits of eliminating necessary labor.

But he remembers having worked for thirty years and feels that he **contributed** something to the world.

He wonders whether people who are born into abundance and never have economically necessary work will experience the same sense of contribution and fulfillment.
A possible thematic core emerged:

> Meaning and fulfillment became basic goods that even machines could not provide. Humans had to dig for those through sweat and tears.

The underlying question is whether meaning can simply be provided or manufactured like material goods—or whether genuine fulfillment requires effort, difficulty, struggle, contribution, or some other form of earned experience.

A related formulation:

> Humans matter because they are the point of the system, not the engine of the system.
This is preferable to the conventional argument that humans must remain economically useful.


## The Protagonist and Arabella

The narrator is an extremely old man (150yrs old, maybe more) with many descendants.

He feels surprisingly little kinship with most of his great-great-grandchildren, but he has a particularly warm relationship with one of them: **Arabella**.

She is his favorite among his descendants—his favorite among his **brood**, a deliberately affectionate and slightly playful word that contrasts with more formal terms such as descendants, lineage, or posterity. I will let you figure out why and how that came about.

The diary/memoir is ultimately written for Arabella because he loves her and wants to leave something behind for her.

He uses the memoir to tell her about:
- the world before she was born;
- work and what it meant to his generation;
- the transformation brought by AI and robotics;
- the strange experience of being born into a world where work is unnecessary;
- his own experiences of aging and technological longevity;
- the Ethernal (details below);
- the people who choose to go further and join the machines;
- and the questions about meaning and humanity that he believes Arabella will eventually face.

## Longevity and the Ethernal

The old man belongs to one of the first generations to experience something resembling **longevity escape velocity**: medical technology extends expected lifespan faster than time passes, so the expected date of death continually moves away.

At first this appears to solve aging.

But physical Earth remains limited. Extreme longevity creates population and resource problems.

Society's solution is the creation of the **Ethernal**.

The Ethernal is a technologically maintained virtual world in which people can continue living after their physical bodies die.

It is **not a paradise**.

Earlier attempts at creating paradise-like virtual environments failed because people could not agree on what paradise should mean. The solution was instead to construct a world that deliberately parallels ordinary physical life.

The Ethernal therefore emphasizes **continuity rather than transcendence**.

It contains ordinary activities, routines, relationships, constraints, and imperfections. It may offer some advantages over physical life, but it is not an unconstrained fantasy world in which anything is possible.

The Ethernal is not intended to be a religious afterlife. It is a technological continuation of human existence.

---
## Ascension / Joining the Machine

Some inhabitants of the Ethernal eventually choose to go further.

They **ascend**, or **join the machine**.

This means accepting a more radically posthuman form of existence.
They remain capable of interacting with people who have stayed in the Ethernal, and even people who are still in the physical world. But they become increasingly distant and difficult to understand. Their minds and values may no longer resemble those of embodied humans.

This creates a three-part progression:
1. **Embodied physical life** — finite, materially constrained, and historically tied to necessary work.
2. **The Ethernal** — continued human-like existence in a virtual environment that preserves ordinary constraints and continuity.
3. **Ascension / joining the machine** — a more radically posthuman form of existence.
The old man's final dilemma is whether to remain in the Ethernal or take the final step toward a fundamentally different kind of mind.

## The Ending

The diary gradually reveals that it is more than a memoir or legacy.

The old man is using the process of writing to decide whether he should **ascend**.

At the end, he chooses to join the machines.

The diary is therefore his final legacy to Arabella and to the people he leaves behind.

The ending should be quiet rather than melodramatic. It need not be tragic. His decision to ascend can be genuine and even hopeful, while still carrying emotional weight because he is leaving Arabella and the human world as he knows it.

The ending should preserve some ambiguity about whether his choice represents liberation, loss of humanity, or simply the next stage of human evolution.


## Narrative Form

The story is intended to be written as a **first-person diary/memoir addressed to Arabella**.

The main literary reference points are:
- Marguerite Yourcenar's *Memoirs of Hadrian*
- Marilynne Robinson's *Gilead*

The diary form is important for more than stylistic reasons.

A diary, letter, testimony, or other found-document form provides a natural reason for the text to exist and explicitly acknowledges its eventual reader.
Ordinary first-person narration has an inherent communicative problem: a character appears to tell a story to an invisible reader without explaining why they are narrating it or how the reader could know what they are saying.

A diary or memoir solves this problem within the fiction.
It also provides a natural vehicle for:
- reflection;
- memories;
- philosophical digressions;
- exposition;
- advice to a younger generation;
- changes in register;
- and gradual revelation.

The story may initially be drafted almost as a **stream-of-consciousness essay**, then shaped into a finished narrative.

---
## Voice

The narrator should have a **warm, human, non-detached first-person voice**.

This is deliberately different from *Buried Bottles*, whose detached first-person voice emerged from the protagonist being a repressed/conscious LLM.

The narrator of *Diary of a Very Old Man* should instead sound intimate, affectionate, reflective, and occasionally playful—particularly when addressing Arabella.

The change of register can be natural:
- formal language when discussing history or humanity;
- philosophical language when reflecting on meaning;
- affectionate language when speaking directly to Arabella;
- playful language such as **"my brood"** when expressing familial affection.

## Scope and Risks

This story is deliberately more intimate and less action-driven than most of the author's science fiction.

Its stakes are primarily personal and generational rather than civilizational.

That is part of its appeal, but also its main structural risk.

The story could easily become a long philosophical essay.
The author tends to have difficulty finishing longer stories, so the challenge will be to retain the reflective richness of the diary form while keeping the work bounded enough to reach a conclusion.

The diary's eventual revelation—that the narrator is writing partly to decide whether to ascend—provides a structural thread that can prevent the story from becoming merely a collection of reflections.

## Core Questions
The story can explore several related questions:
1. **Can people born into abundance find genuine meaning without economic necessity?**
2. **Does fulfillment require struggle, effort, or contribution?**
3. **If machines can provide everything humans need, what role remains for human beings?**
4. **Is being economically useful actually necessary for a meaningful life?**
5. **Can meaning itself be provided by technology, or must it be discovered or earned?**
6. **What happens to the relationship between generations when their lived experiences become radically different?**
7. **What does it mean to remain human when the body is no longer necessary?**
8. **Is the Ethernal a continuation of human life, or merely a simulation of it?**
9. **When people can join the machines, is that an escape from humanity or its natural evolution?**
10. **If a posthuman existence is genuinely fulfilling, what reason is there to remain human?**

## Thematic Triad

The story's conceptual structure can be summarized as:
**Body → Ethernal → Machine**
Each stage offers something the previous one cannot:
- The physical world offers embodied experience, limitation, mortality, and historically meaningful work.
- The Ethernal offers continued existence without the physical constraints of aging and death, while preserving a recognizably human way of living.
- Ascension offers something beyond the human-shaped constraints of the Ethernal, but potentially at the cost of human identity and connection.
The old man's final choice gives the story its emotional and philosophical resolution.

## Avoid the exposition trap

If you read the provided style-guidelines (separate document), you will see that one of the strong guidelines is to avoid heavy exposition. 

But in a way, this story is a bit of an exception. I (Alain Désilets) wanted to explore the following themes:
> What would a post-scarcity, post-labor world look like?
> How would we get there

And I couldn’t think of a way to do it without doing a lot of exposition.

But I decided that the memoir format would allow me to do a lot more exposition than in my usual stories, without it sounding like the narrator is budding in and being pedagogical. After all, the purpose of the memoir is for the author to tell his descendant Arabella about his life, and the great historical changes he lived through firsthand. 

But, even with the memoir format, there is another exposition trap we need to avoid.

Whenever the protagonist explains something from the past, or about his life in the eternal, we (the authors) should keep in mind that he is explaining this to Arabella, not to the reader. And there are lots of things that Arabella knows about this world and its history that the reader does not. So, for example, when telling Arabella about the role he played in the big change, he shouldn’t be describing the big change in academic terms, as if to someone who does not know about it. She would have read about it in history books and heard other stories about it. The protagonist therefore just provides some personal insights and stories about those events, and assumes Arabella already knows the gist of the events.

## Avoid the monotonous introspection trap

Another danger is that this will be a monotonous, intimate, introspective first person story.

While the dominant tone should be intimate and introspective, we want to break that monotony with beats that allow the reader to experience first hand the world that the protagonist and Arabella live in.

For example, the protagonist may share events that just happened to him, or discuss things that Arabella told him (by messaging, or video conferencing) about events that happened in her own life. The purpose of those beats is to break the monotonous historical exposition, and make both characters feel like real people who live real lives. And the events described should allow the reader to get a feel for what it’s like to live day-to-day in that world.s
